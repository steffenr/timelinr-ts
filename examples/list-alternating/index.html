  • The hill is bought

    A bequest from a retired ship's navigator paid for eleven acres of bare hillside, chosen for one reason above all others: on a clear night you could stand there and see nothing at all of the town below.

  • First light

    The twelve-inch refractor saw first light in October.

  • The plate archive begins

    Glass photographic plates replaced the observing notebook, and within a decade the cellar held more than forty thousand of them. Most were catalogued by four women hired as computers, whose initials are still pencilled in the corner of every sleeve; the archive remains the observatory's most consulted holding, and about a fifth of it has still never been digitised.

  • A public night

    The first Thursday opening drew nine visitors. By the end of the season it drew four hundred, and the queue on the access road became a standing complaint at town meetings for the next thirty years.

  • Wartime silence, then radio

    The dome stayed shuttered for the duration and reopened with a surplus antenna array bolted to the east lawn — the first instrument on the site that did not need a clear sky, and the first that could be run in the rain by a single person in a hut.

  • The new spectrograph

    Built in the machine shop over two winters, largely from a drawing that was never finished.

  • Light pollution wins a round

    The town's new sodium lamps put a permanent orange wash across the southern horizon. Deep-sky photography moved to a leased site ninety miles inland, and the hill was reduced, for a while, to teaching and public nights — a period the annual reports of the era describe with conspicuous brevity.

  • The archive goes online

    Six hundred plates were scanned as a pilot, at a resolution that already looked inadequate by the time the pilot ended. The catalogue itself, however, went up complete, and requests arrived from twenty-two countries in the first year.

  • A dark-sky ordinance

    After eleven years of petitioning, the county capped upward light spill. The southern horizon came back — not entirely, and not on damp nights, but enough that the twelve-inch was worth pointing at something faint again.

  • Second century, second telescope

    A one-metre reflector went up in the old radio hut's footprint, remotely operable and booked solid within a month of commissioning. The refractor next door still opens every Thursday, hand-cranked, to whoever walks up the hill.
